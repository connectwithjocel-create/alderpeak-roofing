02 Roofing systems / SHEET M-201

The right roof starts with the right material.

Each roofing system handles Central Texas heat, storms and drainage differently. Select a material to compare where it fits — and where it doesn’t.

Material 01

Asphalt Shingles

Architectural (laminated) asphalt shingles are the default for most pitched residential roofs in Greater Austin. They’re versatile, widely available, and repairable — a good fit when budget and familiarity matter most.

Typical applications
Pitched residential roofs, 4:12 slope and steeper; homes in HOA communities with shingle requirements.
Design character
Dimensional texture with shadow lines; broad range of colors, from weathered wood to charcoal.
Maintenance
Periodic checks of flashing, pipe boots and ridge caps; watch for granule loss and lifted tabs after wind.
Tradeoffs
Shorter service life than metal or tile; Texas heat and hail accelerate wear. Impact-rated shingles cost more.

Material 02

Standing-Seam Metal

Standing-seam roofs use long panels joined by raised, interlocking seams with concealed clips — no exposed fasteners in the field. They suit modern and farmhouse architecture and shed water and heat efficiently.

Typical applications
Residential and light commercial roofs, usually 3:12 slope and up; porches and accent roofs.
Design character
Clean vertical lines; matte and low-gloss painted finishes in charcoal, bronze and galvalume.
Maintenance
Low routine upkeep. Inspect sealant at penetrations, trim and transitions; keep debris clear of valleys.
Tradeoffs
Higher upfront cost; requires specialized installation. Hail can cosmetically dent panels; oil-canning can occur.

Material 03

Tile Roofing

Clay and concrete tile bring a distinctive Mediterranean or Spanish-revival character. The tile itself is extremely durable; the underlayment beneath it does the waterproofing and is the part that ages.

Typical applications
Homes designed for tile, with framing engineered for the added weight; typically 4:12 slope and steeper.
Design character
Barrel and flat profiles; warm terracotta, sand and slate tones; strong shadow patterns.
Maintenance
Replace cracked or slipped tiles; plan underlayment renewal over the roof’s life. Walking on tile can break it.
Tradeoffs
Heavy; not every structure can carry it. Higher material and labor cost; brittle under impact.

Material 04

Commercial Low-Slope

Low-slope roofs rely on continuous membranes rather than overlapping units. Seam quality, drainage design and detailing around rooftop equipment determine how well they perform.

Typical applications
Commercial, retail, office and multifamily buildings; residential additions and porches under 2:12.
Design character
Largely unseen from the street; white reflective membranes can reduce rooftop heat gain.
Maintenance
Semi-annual inspections; keep drains and scuppers clear; check seams, curbs and parapet terminations.
Tradeoffs
Ponding water shortens membrane life; punctures from foot traffic and equipment service are common.

03 Why Alderpeak / SHEET A-301

Roofing is more than what you see from the street.

Shingles and panels are the visible layer. Whether a roof keeps water out for decades depends on the work underneath and at the edges — so that’s where our assessments start.

  1. Assessment before recommendation

    We document conditions on the roof and in the attic before suggesting anything, so recommendations follow evidence.

  2. Material compatibility

    Repairs only hold when new materials work with what’s already there — fasteners, metals, underlayments and sealants included.

  3. Flashing and transitions

    Walls, chimneys, valleys and skylights are where roofs most often leak. They get dedicated attention, not leftover time.

  4. Drainage and ventilation

    Water has to leave the roof and heat has to leave the attic. Both affect how long the roof covering lasts.

  5. Installation planning

    Permits, staging, weather windows and site protection are planned before day one, not improvised on it.

09 Storm protection / SHEET S-701

When the weather turns, your roof matters.

Central Texas sees large hail, straight-line winds and sudden downpours. Damage isn’t always visible from the ground — and small openings let water into decking, insulation and ceilings over time.

Request a Roof Assessment

Stay off the roof after a storm. Wet, damaged or hail-softened surfaces are a serious fall hazard — document what you can from the ground.

Hail
Dark spots where granules are knocked loose, bruised or cracked shingles, dents on metal panels, vents, gutters and downspouts.
Wind
Missing, creased or lifted shingles; exposed underlayment; loosened ridge caps, drip edge or metal trim.
Water
New ceiling stains, damp attic insulation, peeling paint near the roofline, or overflowing gutters during rain.
Debris
Fallen limbs on the roof, granules collecting at downspout outlets, or pieces of shingle and flashing in the yard.

08 Roofing details / SHEET D-501

The details that keep water out.

A roof is a system of parts. These five details decide how well the whole thing performs — and they’re what we look at closely during every assessment.

F-01

Flashing

Metal transitions at walls, chimneys, skylights and valleys. Most leaks we trace start at flashing, not in the open field of the roof.

V-02

Ventilation

Balanced intake at the soffits and exhaust at the ridge moves heat and moisture out of the attic — critical in Texas summers.

U-03

Underlayment

The water-resistant layer between the deck and the roof covering; the backup when wind-driven rain gets past the surface.

D-04

Drainage

Gutters, downspouts, crickets and scuppers move water off and away. Overflow and ponding shorten a roof’s life.

M-05

Roofing materials

Each covering has its own minimum slope, fastening pattern and detailing. Mixing systems requires careful transitions.

12 Frequently asked

Questions, answered plainly.

Straight answers to the questions property owners ask most. If yours isn’t here, send it to us.

Ask a question
What happens during a roof inspection?

We look at the roof covering, flashing at walls, chimneys and penetrations, ridge and valley details, ventilation, gutters and drainage, and — where accessible — the attic for signs of moisture. You get a written summary with photos explaining what we found and what, if anything, needs attention.

How do I know whether to repair or replace?

It depends on how widespread the problem is, the age and condition of the rest of the roof, and whether repair materials will match and seal properly. A localized flashing failure on a sound roof is usually a repair. Widespread granule loss, brittle shingles, or repeated leaks in different areas point toward replacement planning.

Which roofing material is right for Central Texas?

There isn’t one answer. Asphalt shingles, standing-seam metal, tile, and low-slope membranes each suit different budgets, roof pitches, architectural styles, and maintenance expectations. Heat, UV exposure, hail risk, and HOA rules all factor in. Our materials guide walks through the tradeoffs.

Should I have my roof checked after a hailstorm?

If hail or high wind passed through your area, an assessment is a reasonable step even when nothing looks wrong from the ground — bruised shingles and dented metal are often only visible up close. Please don’t climb onto the roof yourself; ladders and wet or damaged surfaces are a real fall risk.

What affects the cost of a roofing project?

Roof size and pitch, the material system, the number of layers to remove, deck repairs discovered during tear-off, flashing and ventilation upgrades, access, and permitting all influence cost. That’s why we price after an on-site assessment rather than quoting from a phone call.

Do you work on commercial properties?

Yes. Commercial and low-slope roofs need different assessments — membrane seams, drainage, rooftop equipment curbs, and parapet details. See commercial roofing for how we approach them.
